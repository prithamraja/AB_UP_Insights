## District-Level Monthly Performance Trends

**Card issuance spikes in Period 3 in 74 of 75 districts — Mahoba shifts to Period 4 instead.**

1. Cards_issued follows the same seasonal peak in **74 of 75 districts**; **Mahoba** is the lone exception with a different highlight.
2. The main peak is concentrated in **2023-05 to 2023-08**, led by **7,345 cards in 2023-07** and **7,342 in 2023-08**; the two lowest observed months are **0 in 2026-02 and 2026-03**.
3. This is a district-level monthly pattern, but it is also echoed at broader levels: **month, quarter, and year** all show rising cumulative beneficiary totals.
4. **Implication:** card issuance drives need tight monthly monitoring in a short seasonal window, with Mahoba treated as a separate operational case rather than assumed to follow the state pattern.

**Claims utilisation jumped in 2023 for 74 of 75 districts — Balrampur is the only district that changed one quarter later.**

1. Claims per 1,000 beneficiaries shows a common change point in **2023Q1 in 74 of 75 districts**; **Balrampur** shifts to **2023Q2**.
2. The district average climbs to **7.52 in 2023Q3** and **7.29 in 2023Q2**, then falls to **4.77 in 2023Q4** and **3.99 in 2024Q1**.
3. The annual view confirms the same story: **2023 peaks at 5.69**, then drops to **3.77 in 2024**, **3.14 in 2025**, and **1.58 in 2026**.
4. **Implication:** utilisation appears to have reset in early 2023 statewide, but Balrampur’s delayed break suggests a district-specific lag in service uptake or reporting.

**PM-JAY payment counts rise in 17 of 18 divisions — Prayagraj breaks the pattern with a different district profile.**

1. Payment count trends upward in **17 of 18 divisions**; **Prayagraj** is the exception.
2. The highest monthly totals occur in **2025-09 (110)** and **2025-03 (108)**, with earlier highs already visible in **2023-08 (102)**.
3. The series starts at **0 in 2022-10** and again hits **0 in 2026-03**, showing that the growth is not smooth across the full period.
4. **Implication:** payment processing capacity has expanded across most divisions, but Prayagraj needs separate review because it does not share the same upward path.

**Average claim size is fairly even across divisions, but Hamirpur and Sambhal sit at the top while Gautam Buddha Nagar and Maharajganj sit at the bottom.**

1. Across **75 districts**, average claim amount ranges from **INR 36,769.37 in Maharajganj** to **INR 60,068.28 in Hamirpur**.
2. Other high-value districts include **Sambhal at INR 57,762.44**, **Shamli at INR 55,432.97**, and **Auraiya at INR 55,044.37**.
3. The division-level pattern is mostly even: **16 of 18 divisions** are classified as even, with **Moradabad** and **Chitrakoot** outside the pattern.
4. **Implication:** average bill size is not a major inter-division outlier, but Hamirpur and Sambhal merit a closer look for case mix, coding, or provider behaviour.

**Claims per 1,000 beneficiaries peaked in 2023 for 65 districts — but 10 districts did not follow the same yearly arc.**

1. At the annual level, **65 of 75 districts** peak in **2023** for claims per 1,000 beneficiaries.
2. The yearly average then declines from **5.69 in 2023** to **3.77 in 2024**, **3.14 in 2025**, and **1.58 in 2026**.
3. The annual pattern is not universal: **Bareilly, Basti, Deoria, Etah, Etawah, Kaushambi, Lakhimpur Kheri, Mathura, Pilibhit, and Sambhal** do not follow the common peak.
4. **Implication:** 2023 looks like the high-water mark for utilisation, but the exceptions should be checked for local service expansion, data gaps, or delayed reporting.

**New beneficiary enrolment falls over time in 65 districts, with 10 districts resisting the decline.**

1. New beneficiaries show a declining quarter-to-quarter trend in **65 of 75 districts**.
2. The strongest quarter volumes are **22,807 in 2023Q2** and **22,642 in 2023Q3**, while the floor is **2 in 2026Q1**.
3. The district exceptions are **Ayodhya, Banda, Etah, Gautam Buddha Nagar, Hapur, Kaushambi, Lucknow, Moradabad, Raebareli, and Unnao**, which do not follow the same downward path.
4. **Implication:** enrolment momentum is weakening statewide, so the exception districts should be checked to see whether they are sustaining outreach better than the rest.

**Division-level beneficiary enrolment also peaks in Period 3 in 15 of 18 divisions — but Azamgarh, Varanasi, and Saharanpur follow a different pattern.**

1. New beneficiaries show the same seasonal pattern in **15 of 18 divisions**, with **Period 3** as the common peak.
2. The main quarterly arc is **22,807 in 2023Q2**, **22,642 in 2023Q3**, and then **20,744 in 2022Q4** before dropping sharply later.
3. **Azamgarh, Varanasi, and Saharanpur** do not match the common seasonal profile.
4. **Implication:** beneficiary mobilisation is concentrated in one seasonal window, so outreach scheduling should be aligned to that period while investigating the three divisions that behave differently.

**Monthly beneficiary enrolment and household enrolment follow the same seasonal and downward movement — with year-level totals breaking the pattern.**

1. At the temporal-grain level, both **month** and **quarter** show **decreasing new beneficiaries** and **Period 3 seasonality**; **year** behaves differently.
2. A parallel seasonality appears for **new households** as well: **month** and **quarter** peak in **Period 3**, while **year** is the exception.
3. This means the enrolment slowdown is not isolated to one metric; it appears in both **new beneficiaries** and **new households**.
4. **Implication:** enrolment management should be planned around the monthly/quarterly cycle, not annual summaries, because annual aggregation hides the seasonal falloff.

**Hospitals, claims, payments, and utilisation all turned upward from 2023Q1 — while beneficiary enrolment turned downward.**

1. At the measure level, **17 of 26 indicators** shift at **2023Q1**, including **cases admitted, emergency cases, public/private cases, claims submitted, claims approved, amount claimed, amount paid, payment count, claims per 1,000 beneficiaries, and public-private ratio**.
2. In contrast, **new beneficiaries** and **new households** shift to **a decreasing trend**, not the upward one seen in service and payment metrics.
3. **Payments failures** and **cards issued** shift later, in **2025Q3**, while **deaths** and **amount approved/paid/payment count** shift at **2023Q2** in part of the measure set.
4. **Implication:** service use and financial flows accelerated before enrolment did, suggesting the system became more active on the delivery side while new enrolment softened.

**The same split shows up in divisions: service volumes rise, but beneficiary enrolment falls in 65 districts and 15 divisions.**

1. Division-level measure trends are mixed: **cases admitted, emergency cases, deaths, claims, payments, and public-private ratio** rise in **15 of 26 measures**, while **new beneficiaries** and **new households** fall.
2. The common change point for many operational metrics is **2023Q1**, but **new beneficiaries, new households, and cards_issued** break later at **2025Q3**.
3. **Payment failures** also shift at **2023Q2**, reinforcing that payment operations have their own timing distinct from enrolment.
4. **Implication:** enrolment, claims, and payment functions are not moving together; operational oversight should track them separately rather than treating them as one pipeline.

**Beneficiary enrolment and households both fall in 65 districts, but Lucknow and nearby divisions still show the same seasonal recruitment pulse.**

1. At district-quarter level, **65 of 75 districts** show **decreasing new beneficiaries**, with the strongest quarters in **2023Q2 and 2023Q3**.
2. The division-level pattern mirrors this: **15 of 18 divisions** follow **Period 3 seasonality** for new beneficiaries and new households.
3. **Lucknow** appears in the common seasonal set for both **new beneficiaries** and **new households**, showing that the metro does not diverge from the broader pattern.
4. **Implication:** beneficiary drives are still seasonal and concentrated, so monthly district plans should focus on the same quarter where enrolment historically responds.

**Lucknow’s new household pattern is ordinary, but the district-level peak shifts from 2023Q2 to 2023Q3 in 7 divisions, including Agra and Gorakhpur.**

1. For **new households**, the common peak is **2023Q2** in **11 of 18 divisions**.
2. The exception set moves the peak to **2023Q3** in **7 divisions**, including **Agra, Azamgarh, Chitrakoot, Varanasi, Gorakhpur, Prayagraj, and Mirzapur**.
3. The quarterly volume is largest at **21,394 in 2023Q2** and **21,173 in 2023Q3**, with the floor at **452 in 2025Q4**.
4. **Implication:** household enrolment campaigns should be timed differently in the seven exception divisions rather than using a single statewide calendar.

**Lucknow’s hospital network behaves like the rest of the state, with month and quarter following the same seasonal pattern.**

1. In the Lucknow division, **unique hospitals** show **Period 3 seasonality** at both **month** and **quarter** levels.
2. The year dimension is the only exception, so the pattern is stable across the two finer time scales.
3. This matches the broader state finding that seasonality is common across most measures and temporal grains.
4. **Implication:** provider participation in Lucknow is tied to the same seasonal rhythm as beneficiary activity, so hospital engagement should be planned around the same peak period.

**Meerut shows the same upward service pattern in admissions and beneficiary stock, with month and quarter moving together.**

1. In the Meerut division, **cases admitted** increase over both **month** and **quarter**.
2. **Cumulative beneficiaries** in Meerut also follow **Period 3 seasonality** at both temporal levels.
3. This aligns with the broader statewide trend where service-side measures rise while beneficiary enrolment declines.
4. **Implication:** Meerut looks operationally active on both utilisation and enrolment stock, so it should be monitored as a high-throughput division rather than a slow one.

**Varanasi is split between more portability and more private care, but both still follow the same monthly/quarterly rhythm.**

1. In **Varanasi**, **portability cases** rise over time, while **private cases** show **Period 3 seasonality**.
2. Both measures behave the same way at **month** and **quarter** levels, with **year** as the only exception.
3. The state-level measure set also shows portability as a later change point, separating it from the main 2023 pattern.
4. **Implication:** Varanasi’s case mix is shifting toward mobility and private utilisation, so portability and private-provider tracking should be watched together.

**Prayagraj’s card issuance falls over time, but its payment count still follows the normal seasonal pulse.**

1. In the Prayagraj division, **cards_issued** shows a **decreasing trend** at both **month** and **quarter** levels.
2. At the same time, **payment_count** follows **Period 3 seasonality**, so utilisation still clusters seasonally.
3. Prayagraj is also the division that breaks the otherwise rising payment-count trend at the division level.
4. **Implication:** Prayagraj may be losing momentum on card issuance even while its claim settlement activity remains seasonally active, so outreach and usage need to be reviewed separately.

**Kanpur’s emergency load and approved amounts both rise with the same seasonal rhythm.**

1. In **Kanpur**, **emergency cases** increase over both **month** and **quarter**.
2. **Amount approved** also shows **Period 3 seasonality** across the same time grains.
3. This fits the wider state pattern where emergency and payment-related metrics are moving upward together.
4. **Implication:** Kanpur should be monitored as a high-pressure division where emergency admissions and financial outgo rise in tandem.

**Bareilly’s LAMA/DAMA exits are rising over time, while Moradabad’s public admissions and public/private mix are also climbing.**

1. In **Bareilly**, **LAMA/DAMA cases** increase across **month** and **quarter**.
2. In **Moradabad**, **public cases** increase across the same time grains.
3. The district/month patterns elsewhere also show common seasonality in public-private mix and payment volumes, so these are not isolated shifts.
4. **Implication:** Bareilly needs review for discharge behaviour, while Moradabad should be checked for whether its rising public case load is stressing public facilities.

**The state’s service metrics are mostly trending up, but claims per 1,000 beneficiaries and cards issued are the two main counters that break later.**

1. At measure level, **15 of 26 measures** trend upward over **month/quarter**, including admissions, emergency cases, claims, payments, and cumulative beneficiaries.
2. **Claims per 1,000 beneficiaries** is not part of the upward set and is instead a later change-point measure.
3. **Cards_issued** also breaks later, shifting at **2025Q3** rather than joining the early 2023 inflection.
4. **Implication:** the state should treat claim intensity and card issuance as delayed signals, not assume they moved in step with the rest of the system.

## Hospital Infrastructure & Specialty Capacity

**Cardiology and orthopaedics absorb 65.6% of specialty spend in 74 of 75 districts — with Shamli the only district where obstetrics joins the top two.**

1. Statewide, Cardiology (CARD) accounts for INR 40.04 crore (39.9%) and Orthopaedics (ORTH) INR 25.78 crore (25.7%) of the INR 100.27 crore specialty spend.
2. This top-two pattern appears in 74 of 75 districts (98.7%); the lone exception is Shamli.
3. In Shamli, the top two shift to Cardiology and Obstetrics & Gynaecology (OBG) instead of Orthopaedics.
4. Implication: the Shamli mix is worth checking for referral flows, specialty availability, or coding behaviour that differs from the rest of UP.

**Specialist experience is almost flat across the state, with Agra the only division that breaks the pattern.**

1. Average clinical experience stays in a narrow 16.58–18.32 years band across specialties, with Paediatrics (PEDS) and Oncology (ONCO) highest at 18.32 years.
2. Burns (BURNS) is lowest at 16.58 years, but the spread is only 1.74 years across all 15 specialties.
3. The same evenness holds in 17 of 18 divisions (94.4%); Agra is the only division that departs from the flat pattern.
4. Implication: experience levels look broadly uniform, so any performance gaps are less likely to be explained by staffing seniority alone.

**Small hospitals deliver the highest PM-JAY cases per bed in every division except Jhansi, where the pattern shifts.**

1. Hospitals with 30 beds or fewer average 0.20 cases per bed, versus 0.05 in 31–100 bed hospitals, 0.02 in 101–300 bed hospitals, and 0.01 in 300+ bed hospitals.
2. This small-hospital advantage appears in 17 of 18 divisions (94.4%); Jhansi is the exception.
3. Jhansi breaks from the standard bed-size pattern, indicating a different utilisation profile than the rest of the state.
4. Implication: smaller facilities are carrying the heaviest inpatient load per bed, so bed and staffing pressure should be monitored there first.

**Admissions before last year are concentrated in large hospitals, while burns and dermatology do not follow the same bed-size pattern.**

1. Large hospitals (101–300 beds) account for 28.49 lakh admissions before last year, or 41.3% of the total 68.94 lakh.
2. Medium hospitals add 22.66 lakh (32.9%), small hospitals 13.48 lakh (19.5%), and very large hospitals only 4.32 lakh (6.3%).
3. The expected bed-size pattern holds for 13 of 15 specialties (86.7%), but Burns (BURNS) and Dermatology (DERM) are the exceptions.
4. Implication: historic admissions are heavily skewed toward mid-sized and large facilities, but burns and dermatology need separate planning because they behave differently.

**Cardiology alone drives four in ten approved rupees in large hospitals, with orthopaedics the only close follower.**

1. In large hospitals, Cardiology (CARD) contributes INR 12.72 crore (40.2%) of approved amounts, and Orthopaedics (ORTH) INR 8.02 crore (25.3%).
2. Together, these two specialties make up 65.5% of the INR 31.66 crore approved in this hospital-size band.
3. The same Cardiology-first pattern holds across all 8 hospital sub-types in large hospitals, with no exceptions.
4. Implication: approval pressure is heavily concentrated in a few high-value specialties, so these service lines deserve the closest audit and capacity oversight.

**Very large hospitals account for the fewest treated cases, and that low-volume pattern is shared by almost all specialties.**

1. Very large hospitals (300+ beds) treat only 1,476 cases, just 6.7% of the 21,924 cases recorded.
2. Large hospitals lead with 8,940 cases (40.8%), followed by medium hospitals at 7,139 (32.6%) and small hospitals at 4,369 (19.9%).
3. The same last-place pattern for very large hospitals appears in 11 of 15 specialties (73.3%).
4. Burns (BURNS), Dermatology (DERM), Nephrology (NEPHRO), and Psychiatry (PSYCH) do not fit this pattern.
5. Implication: very large hospitals are not the main PM-JAY case engine in most specialties, so low throughput there should not be mistaken for a statewide norm.

**Zero-claim private hospitals are overwhelmingly large, and obstetrics is the one specialty that breaks away.**

1. Among private hospitals, large facilities account for 127 zero-claim hospital-specialty pairs, or 70.2% of the 181 zero-claim instances.
2. Medium private hospitals contribute 32 (17.7%), and small private hospitals 22 (12.2%).
3. This large-hospital concentration holds across 14 of 15 specialties (93.3%); Obstetrics & Gynaecology (OBG) is the exception.
4. Implication: when private hospitals go unused, it is usually the larger facilities, so zero-claim review should start with them rather than smaller providers.

**Public hospitals keep expired licence counts even across bed-size bands, with small hospitals only slightly higher.**

1. In public hospitals, expired licences average 3.75 in small facilities, 3.66 in medium, 3.40 in very large, and 3.33 in large hospitals.
2. The full spread is only 0.42 licences, so no bed-size band stands out sharply.
3. This even pattern holds across 13 of 15 specialties (86.7%); Burns (BURNS) and Nephrology (NEPHRO) do not follow it.
4. Implication: expired-licence burden looks broadly systemic rather than size-specific, so compliance action should be hospital-wide.

**Private large hospitals are the only bed-size group where zero claims concentrate, and OBG is the outlier specialty.**

1. In private hospitals, large facilities account for 127 of 181 zero-claim cases, or 70.2%.
2. Medium and small private hospitals are far lower at 32 (17.7%) and 22 (12.2%) respectively.
3. The same large-hospital concentration appears in 14 of 15 specialties (93.3%); OBG is the exception.
4. Implication: unclaimed specialty capacity in private hospitals is mostly a large-hospital issue, not a widespread issue across all sizes.

**Public hospitals with small beds generate the highest cases per bed in every division.**

1. Public hospitals with 30 beds or fewer average 0.22 cases per bed, compared with 0.05 in 31–100 bed hospitals, 0.02 in 101–300 bed hospitals, and 0.01 in 300+ bed hospitals.
2. This is not a partial pattern: all 18 divisions follow it.
3. The same small-hospital dominance also holds across specialties in public hospitals, with no exceptions in the division-level view.
4. Implication: small public hospitals are under the greatest utilisation pressure statewide, so bed planning and staffing support should start there.

**Public hospitals keep total beds low in every specialty, but the state’s total bed stock is concentrated in large and very large facilities.**

1. Across public hospitals, specialty-wise total bed strength is always lowest in small hospitals, at 20,872 beds or just 4.3% of the 4.87 lakh total.
2. Large hospitals hold 1.89 lakh beds (38.9%), very large hospitals 1.63 lakh (33.5%), and medium hospitals 1.14 lakh (23.3%).
3. This small-hospital last-place pattern holds for all 15 specialties (100%).
4. Implication: small public facilities have the least physical capacity but the highest cases per bed, which is a clear congestion signal.

**Public hospitals show the same small-bed pressure across every district, with only a few districts moving toward medium beds.**

1. In public hospitals, small-bed facilities average 0.22 cases per bed, versus 0.05 for medium, 0.02 for large, and 0.01 for very large facilities.
2. All 75 districts follow this small-hospital lead except a handful that shift toward medium beds: Balrampur, Lalitpur, and Pilibhit.
3. The pattern is otherwise universal, with 62 districts (82.7%) still anchored in the small-bed highlight.
4. Implication: district-level bed pressure is a public-hospital-wide issue, but Balrampur, Lalitpur, and Pilibhit need a closer look for different capacity structure.

**Large private hospitals dominate the full set of activity metrics, but cases per bed is the one measure that shifts to small hospitals.**

1. In private hospitals, large facilities lead 14 of 20 measures (70%), including admissions, claims, amounts paid, emergency counts, and zero-claim flags.
2. The main exception is cases per bed, which moves to small hospitals instead of large ones.
3. Staffing and licence measures also break away from the large-hospital pattern, showing that not every operational metric behaves like volume.
4. Implication: large private hospitals are the main volume centres, but small hospitals are the more intense users of each bed.

**Amount approved in large public hospitals is led by Cardiology and Orthopaedics everywhere, with no sub-type exceptions.**

1. In public hospitals with 101–300 beds, Cardiology (CARD) accounts for INR 12.72 crore (40.2%) of approved amounts and Orthopaedics (ORTH) INR 8.02 crore (25.3%).
2. The next specialties are much smaller: OBG at INR 2.58 crore (8.2%), GS at INR 2.31 crore (7.3%), and URO at INR 1.39 crore (4.4%).
3. All 8 hospital sub-types follow this same CARD-ORTH ranking, with no exceptions.
4. Implication: approved spend in large public hospitals is tightly concentrated, making these two specialties the main focus for claims oversight.

**Public hospital expiry counts are even across specialties and bed sizes, with no one specialty driving the problem.**

1. In public hospitals, expired licence counts are nearly flat across bed-size bands: 3.75 in small, 3.66 in medium, 3.40 in very large, and 3.33 in large facilities.
2. Specialty-wise, the pattern is even across 13 of 15 specialties (86.7%), with only Burns and Nephrology outside the norm.
3. The difference between the highest and lowest bed-size averages is only 0.42.
4. Implication: expired licences are a broad compliance issue, not a problem concentrated in one specialty or one hospital size.

**Cases per bed in private hospitals is driven by small facilities across nearly all service lines, even though large hospitals dominate most other activity measures.**

1. Private hospitals with 30 beds or fewer average 0.22 cases per bed, compared with 0.05 in medium hospitals, 0.02 in large hospitals, and 0.01 in very large hospitals.
2. This small-hospital lead appears in 14 of 20 private-hospital measures, including most admissions and claims indicators.
3. Cases per bed is the standout shift: it moves away from the large-hospital pattern and sits with the small hospitals instead.
4. Implication: high volume and high intensity are not the same thing in private facilities; the smallest hospitals are the busiest per bed.

**Orthopaedics and Cardiology take the largest share of paid amounts in private hospitals, regardless of bed size.**

1. In private hospitals, Cardiology (CARD) accounts for INR 1.73 crore (37.0%) of paid amounts and Orthopaedics (ORTH) INR 1.15 crore (24.6%).
2. Together they make up 61.6% of the INR 4.68 crore paid in this bed-size group.
3. General Surgery (GS) is a distant third at INR 42.54 lakh (9.1%).
4. Implication: the payment profile in private hospitals is highly concentrated, so these two specialties should be the main targets for claim reconciliation.

**Public hospitals with small beds have the highest cases per bed in every division, with no district-level exception.**

1. Public small hospitals average 0.22 cases per bed, compared with 0.05 in medium, 0.02 in large, and 0.01 in very large facilities.
2. Every one of the 18 divisions follows this same ranking.
3. There are no exceptions in the division-level public view.
4. Implication: this is a state-wide pressure point in public facilities, not a localised anomaly.

**Private hospitals keep large-facility dominance across most measures, but staffing and licence counts do not follow the same size pattern.**

1. Large private hospitals lead 14 of 20 measures, including admissions, claims, amounts, emergencies, deaths, and zero claims.
2. Cases per bed is the key reversal, shifting to small hospitals rather than large ones.
3. Total staff, average experience, total licences, expired licences, and active licences all behave differently from the volume measures.
4. Implication: capacity, staffing, and utilisation need to be reviewed together in private hospitals; volume alone does not explain operating pressure.

**Cardiology and orthopaedics dominate approved amounts in large private hospitals just as they do in large public hospitals.**

1. In large private hospitals, Cardiology (CARD) contributes INR 16.48 crore (40.1%) of approved amounts and Orthopaedics (ORTH) INR 10.57 crore (25.7%).
2. The next specialties are far smaller: OBG at INR 3.37 crore (8.2%) and GS at INR 2.98 crore (7.3%).
3. This pattern is seen across 5 of 8 hospital sub-types, with PHC, Medical College, and NGO breaking away.
4. Implication: approved claims in large private hospitals are concentrated in the same two specialties as public hospitals, so these lines should be managed as the core cost drivers.

**Public large hospitals are the opposite of small ones: they have the most beds, but the weakest utilisation per bed.**

1. In public hospitals, large facilities hold 1.89 lakh beds (38.9%) and very large facilities 1.63 lakh beds (33.5%).
2. Yet cases per bed are lowest there, at 0.02 in large hospitals and 0.01 in very large hospitals, compared with 0.22 in small hospitals.
3. The small-hospital lead is universal across all 15 specialties and all districts in the public view.
4. Implication: bed expansion has not translated into proportional utilisation in large public hospitals, so capacity deployment should be reviewed.

**Large private hospitals are where most zero-claim activity sits, while small and medium hospitals contribute far fewer empty specialty slots.**

1. Among private hospitals, large facilities account for 127 zero-claim combinations, or 70.2% of the 181 total.
2. Medium facilities contribute 32 (17.7%), and small facilities 22 (12.2%).
3. This pattern holds across 14 of 15 specialties, with only OBG behaving differently.
4. Implication: if a private hospital is carrying unused specialty approvals, it is most likely a large facility, not a small one.

**Public hospitals keep licence expiry levels stable across bed sizes, with small facilities only marginally higher.**

1. Expired licences average 3.75 in small public hospitals, 3.66 in medium, 3.40 in very large, and 3.33 in large hospitals.
2. The spread is narrow at just 0.42, so the issue is not concentrated in one size band.
3. Specialty-wise, 13 of 15 specialties match the same even pattern.
4. Implication: licence renewal discipline needs to be handled as a routine system task rather than a targeted size-specific intervention.

**Private hospitals show a consistent service mix in large facilities, but cases per bed shifts the pressure to small hospitals.**

1. Large private hospitals dominate 14 of 20 measures, including admissions, claims, amounts, emergencies, deaths, and zero claims.
2. Cases per bed is the clear exception, where small hospitals are the highest at 0.22 versus 0.05 in medium and 0.02 in large hospitals.
3. Staffing and licence measures also do not follow the large-facility pattern.
4. Implication: utilisation management in private hospitals should focus on small facilities, while volume and billing control should focus on large ones.

## Beneficiary Enrolment & Scheme Uptake

**SECC beneficiaries account for 60.2% of claims in 74 of 75 districts — except Lalitpur, which breaks the source mix.**

1. Statewide claim volume from entitlement sources is heavily SECC-led at 13,547 claims (60.2%), versus 5,630 from the state database (25.0%) and 3,323 from RSBY (14.8%).
2. This SECC pattern appears in 74 of 75 districts; Lalitpur is the only district that does not follow it.
3. The same source mix also holds among active beneficiaries, where SECC contributes 11,866 claims (60.3%), compared with 4,927 from the state database and 2,884 from RSBY.
4. Implication: Lalitpur should be checked for a different enrolment pipeline, legacy-data dependence, or source tagging issue.

**Card issuance timing is almost identical across active, inactive, and disabled cards — and Chitrakoot is the only district that does not fit the pattern.**

1. Average enrolment-to-card time is 15.48 days for ACTIVE cards, 15.37 for INACTIVE, and 15.22 for DISABLED cards.
2. The spread is only 0.26 days across all three card statuses, so card status is not driving the delay.
3. This evenness holds in 74 of 75 districts; Chitrakoot is the lone district that behaves differently.
4. Implication: the issue looks systemwide rather than status-specific, so local process checks should focus on enrolment-to-issuance workflow rather than card category.

**SHA approvals and rejected enrolments together shape claim patterns in 17 of 18 divisions — except Mirzapur, where the approval mix differs.**

1. Across divisions, claim count by enrolment status is led by ISA_APPROVED at 7,635 claims (33.9%) and AUTO_APPROVED at 7,404 (32.9%).
2. SHA_APPROVED contributes 5,262 claims (23.4%) and REJECTED still adds 2,199 claims (9.8%).
3. The joint pattern of SHA_APPROVED and REJECTED appearing together holds in 17 of 18 divisions; Mirzapur is the exception.
4. Implication: Mirzapur may need a closer look at how approvals are being routed and whether local enrolment decisions differ from the state norm.

**Rejected enrolments still file claims in 68 of 75 districts — except Lalitpur, Amethi, Etawah, Chitrakoot, Shravasti, Kannauj and Balrampur.**

1. REJECTED enrolments generate 2,107 claims statewide, which is 9.9% of all claims in this view.
2. This REJECTED pattern is present in 68 districts, but 7 districts do not follow it: Lalitpur, Amethi, Etawah, Chitrakoot, Shravasti, Kannauj and Balrampur.
3. The same pattern is also seen among all beneficiaries, where REJECTED accounts for 2,199 claims (9.8%).
4. Implication: these seven districts should be checked for post-rejection card use, status corrections, or data lag between approval and utilisation.

**SECC beneficiaries drive claims in 67 of 75 districts — with Lalitpur, Muzaffarnagar, Gautam Buddha Nagar, Kanpur Dehat, Chitrakoot, Agra, Aligarh and Sant Ravidas Nagar breaking the rule.**

1. SECC contributes 13,547 claims statewide, or 60.2% of all claims by entitlement source.
2. The SECC claim pattern holds in 67 districts, but 8 districts deviate: Lalitpur, Muzaffarnagar, Gautam Buddha Nagar, Kanpur Dehat, Chitrakoot, Agra, Aligarh and Sant Ravidas Nagar.
